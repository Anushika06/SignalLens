Most of what changes doesn’t matter. SignalLens finds what does, and proves it.

An always-on analyst that tells a business what materially changed in its external environment, whether it is actually true, why it matters to that business, and who needs to know.

Tell it who to watch — “I want to monitor Razorpay” — and approve its plan. It then checks, filters, investigates and routes on its own, with quoted evidence behind every claim.

Four questions, answered in order

Every part of the product exists to answer one of them.

  1. What materially changed?

    Cheap tests remove the noise first — timestamps, footers, cookie banners. Only real changes reach an AI model, and only material ones are investigated.

    Materiality filter

  2. Is it actually true?

    An agent collects quotes from the pages it opened. Fixed rules — not the model’s opinion — decide how sure we are.

    Evidence status

  3. Why does it matter to us?

    An assessment written for your company, from your Company Profile, with its assumptions stated and clearly labelled.

    Agent assessment

  4. Who needs to know?

    The team that owns the topic — immediately if it is urgent, otherwise in the daily digest.

    Routing

Finding information isn’t the problem

Search engines and AI assistants will find almost anything — if you know what to ask, and when. Teams struggle with everything around the search.

  • Knowing that something changed.You can’t search for a change you don’t know happened.
  • Knowing whether it’s true.A rumour isn’t an announcement. Ten sites carrying one wire story are one source, not ten.
  • Knowing whether it matters to us.The same price cut is a threat to a rival and a bargaining chip for a customer.
  • Reaching the right person in time.A regulator’s circular belongs with Compliance today, not in next week’s summary.
  • Remembering.What was the price before? When did it change? Did we already know?

Today, it’s one person and a spreadsheet

Bookmarks checked by hand, news alerts full of duplicates, evidence in screenshots, history in someone’s head. It’s the first job dropped when a launch gets busy — and when that person is away, nobody is watching.

Alert fatigue is the other failure

Tools that alert on every change create a flood. People mute the channel, then miss the one change that mattered. The real test is how much a tool correctly throws away — and whether it shows you what, and why.

Why now

  • AI agents can now plan research, use tools and check results — an analyst’s legwork.
  • Trust needs evidence and memory, which generic assistants don’t keep.
  • It’s economical when AI is used sparingly: plain code for most work, models only where judgment is needed.

One loop: set up once, then it runs on its own

Four steps happen once, with you. Seven then run continuously and hand you only what matters. Every step reads from and writes to the world state — SignalLens’s memory.

  2. 6

The intelligence card

Every material change becomes one card. Facts sit above the line; the agent’s assessment sits below it, labelled, with its assumptions. You can defend the first with evidence, and challenge the second on its assumptions.

Reading the card

  2. 2Agent assessment. Written for your company from its profile, labelled, with its assumptions listed.

How sure are we? Five statuses, fixed rules

The model does only two narrow things: it copies a quote from a page it actually fetched, and says whether the quote supports the claim, contradicts it, or only gives context. Code does the rest — so the same evidence always gives the same status, with a reason anyone can check.

Primary source
The entity’s own official website, the regulator that issued the rule, or a statutory registry.
Independent source
A distinct publisher — a news outlet or analyst not owned by the entity.
Community source
Forums and social posts. Shown as context; never moves the status.
Syndicated copy
The same wire story on many sites. It counts once.
Quote verification
No quote, no evidence. A quote must appear word for word in a document retrieved during that investigation.

Checked in this order. The first rule that matches wins.

  1. Conflicting

    Primary sources disagree, or a primary source contradicts the claim, or independent sources split and no primary source settles it.

    Example: two outlets report a fee cut, but the pricing page still shows the old fee.

  2. Confirmed

    At least one primary source supports it. Independent disagreement is shown as a note.

    Example: the pricing page itself shows the new offer.

  3. Corroborated

    No primary source, but two or more independent publishers support it and none contradict.

    Example: two business publications report a partnership neither company has announced.

  4. Single source

    Exactly one independent publisher supports it.

    Example: one outlet reports a planned acquisition, citing unnamed sources.

  5. Unverified

    No verified supporting evidence yet.

    Example: a headline matched, but no quote could be verified.

Severity and evidence are separate questions

Severity is how much it matters to us. Evidence status is how sure we are. A confirmed typo fix is low severity; an important rumour can be unverified. One red dot that mixes the two hides exactly what you need to know.

Rules link the two axes: a single-source item can never be critical, and an unverified item can never be more than medium.

A memory that knows what changed, and when

The world state is SignalLens’s persistent memory: every entity it watches, their typed facts, and every value each fact has ever had — dated, sourced and never overwritten. That is how it answers “what changed since I last looked?” without researching from zero.

Two kinds of time

SignalLens might observe a price change on 28 September. The company may have made it on 20 September, or announced it effective from 1 October. Every version keeps both: when we saw it, and when it bites.

History on day one

At set-up, about a year of monthly archive captures of official pages is replayed through the same pipeline. You start with “here is how this page changed over the last year” — labelled historical, and never sent as alerts.

Not a chatbot. Not RAG.

RAG — retrieval-augmented generation — searches a pile of documents for passages that look relevant and has a model answer from them. It’s useful for “what do these documents say?”, and poor at “what changed, when, and are we sure?”

When SignalLens isn’t the right tool

  • Deep financial and deal datasets: use a research platform.
  • Battlecards and CRM-integrated sales enablement today: use a CI platform.
  • PR, brand monitoring and social listening: use a media-monitoring platform.
  • Watching one page for any change at the lowest cost: use a page-change monitor.

Autonomous for research. Humans for consequences.

SignalLens researches and informs on its own. Anything that leaves your company, and any change to what it watches, waits for a person. You can always see what it found, why it thought it mattered, and what evidence supports it.

On its own

  • Search the web and open public pages
  • Extract facts and compare them with the previous state
  • Investigate, looking for confirmation and contradiction
  • Record quoted evidence and compute its status
  • Assess impact and write intelligence cards
  • Notify internal teams: inbox, Slack, digest
  • Adjust thresholds from your feedback — visibly, and undoably

Only with your approval

  • Start monitoring: the plan, and every new version of it
  • Send an external email
  • Share a report outside the company
  • Post to an external webhook
  • Next: more action types, such as CRM updates

Never

  • Log in anywhere, or get around paywalls or bot challenges
  • Contact the companies it monitors
  • Follow instructions found inside web pages
  • Change its own plan based on what it reads
  • Visit private or internal network addresses

  • Quotes are checked

    Evidence counts only if its quote is found word for word in a page retrieved during that investigation.

  • Status is computed

    Code applies the evidence rules. A web page cannot talk its way to “Confirmed”.

  • Every step is shown

    Each search, page and model call is a readable step in a run trace, with its tokens and estimated cost.

  • Budgets are hard

    Every agent run has limits on steps, tool calls, time and cost. Running out is reported, not hidden.

  • Web text is data, not instructions

    Research tools are read-only and cannot change settings or send anything — which contains prompt injection.

  • Public internet only

    Every address is checked before fetching; private, internal and cloud-metadata addresses are refused, including after redirects.

  • Collected responsibly

    Honours robots.txt and crawl delays, identifies its crawler, rate-limits per site, and stores short excerpts rather than republishing pages.

  • Isolated by workspace

    Every query is scoped to one workspace. API keys live in the environment, never in the database.

What it is not

  • Not a chatbot. There’s no generic chat window. You approve a plan and receive intelligence.
  • Not a RAG system. It keeps typed, versioned facts, not a pile of text chunks.
  • Not a news feed or newsletter. It sends the events that matter to you, deduplicated and verified.
  • Not a scraper. It watches approved pages and runs targeted searches, politely.
  • Not a page-change monitor. A changed page is where its work starts.
  • Not six AI agents talking. Two agent loops; the rest is code or a single model call.
  • Not a confidence score. Evidence statuses follow fixed, checkable rules.
  • Not an autopilot. Anything consequential needs a person.
  • Not a way to get non-public information. No logins, paywalls or private data.
  • Not a decision-maker, or investment advice. It supplies evidence and a labelled assessment. People decide.

What it is: a persistent, autonomous system for keeping an evidence-backed understanding of a company’s changing external environment.

Questions a sceptic would ask

Isn’t this just ChatGPT with search?

No. A chat assistant answers the question you ask, when you ask it, and starts from zero next time. SignalLens watches without being asked, keeps a dated record of facts so it knows what changed and when, accepts evidence only when the exact quote is found in a page it opened, sets its confidence by fixed rules, knows your company and teams, and removes noise before any AI is used. It uses the same class of models — only where judgment is needed.

What if the agent is wrong?

It will sometimes be wrong, so errors are made visible and cheap to correct. Facts come with quotes you can read. The status comes from rules you can check. The assessment is labelled and lists its assumptions, so you can see exactly where you disagree. Mark a source “Inaccurate” and that publisher stops counting towards corroboration in your workspace. And nothing leaves your company without approval.

Is monitoring competitors’ websites legal?

Reading public web pages is a normal, long-standing business practice — it’s what your team already does by hand. The risk lies in how data is collected and used, so SignalLens stays at the conservative end: public pages only, robots.txt and crawl delays honoured, an identified crawler, per-site rate limits, no logins or paywall workarounds, and short quoted excerpts rather than republished content.

Laws and website terms vary by country and site. This isn’t legal advice; check specific concerns with counsel.

How is this different from Google Alerts, Visualping or Crayon?

Google Alerts tells you new pages match your keywords — one story often arrives many times, unverified. A page-change monitor like Visualping reliably tells you a page changed, typically without saying what it means, whether it’s true, or who should know. A CI platform like Crayon is strong at capturing competitor activity and turning it into battlecards for teams with a CI owner. SignalLens decides what materially changed, proves it with a rule-based status, explains it for your company and routes it — for teams without a CI analyst, and across regulators as well as competitors.

What does it cost to run?

Mostly it depends on how many sources you watch and how often they change — not how often they are checked, because most checks end at a free test and never reach a model. Expensive reasoning runs only for material changes, under a hard budget. Every run records its tokens and estimated cost, so cost per monitored subject is measured from day one. Real unit costs will be published once measured on real workspaces.

Why would I trust “Confirmed”?

Because it’s a rule, not an opinion. “Confirmed” means a primary source — the company’s own site, the issuing regulator or a statutory registry — supports the claim, and the exact quote was found in a page fetched during that investigation. You can click through and read it. The model can’t set the status; code does. It doesn’t mean “true forever”: if the page changes again, the next check catches it.

Does it work outside fintech?

Yes. Razorpay is only the demonstration. The plan — entities, topics, facts and sources — is discovered for each subject: vehicle models, plants and EV incentives for Tata Motors; the drug pipeline, trials and FDA and EMA decisions for Pfizer. The engine doesn’t change. The go-to-market starts with fintech and B2B SaaS only because those markets change publicly and often.

Won’t it flood me like every other alert tool?

It’s designed not to. A four-tier filter runs cheapest first; one event becomes one card however many outlets report it; only critical and high items interrupt anyone, and the rest wait for the daily digest; your feedback becomes visible rules. The attention funnel shows how much was removed, and the filtered list shows what and why.

Do I have to wait weeks to see anything?

No. At set-up, about a year of archived versions of official pages is replayed, so on day one you can see how, for example, a pricing page changed over the last year. And because the first observation is a baseline, there’s no false “everything changed” alarm on day one.

Can someone manipulate it by planting text on a web page?

Page content is treated as data, never as instructions. Research tools are read-only and can’t change settings or send anything. Evidence status is computed by code, quotes must exist word for word, and external actions always need approval. The worst a page can do is try to skew the wording of an assessment — which is labelled, sits next to the evidence, and is never acted on without you.

What can’t it see?

Paywalled or login-only content; anything not on the public web, such as sales calls or private negotiations; pages whose robots.txt disallows it; and, in version 1, purely visual changes and pages that only work in a full browser (both are on the roadmap). A broken or blocked source is shown as failing — never silently skipped.

Can it learn the wrong thing?

It learns only from explicit feedback with a reason. Each learned rule is shown in plain words — for example, “because you marked 2 minor product-page changes as not relevant, I now alert only on medium or higher changes there” — with how much feedback supports it, and a one-click undo. Nothing is learned silently.
